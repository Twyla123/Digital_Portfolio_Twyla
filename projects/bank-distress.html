Evaluating Bank Distress from Regulatory and Market Data

Built part of the data layer for a study of whether brokered deposits make bank funding less stable, since published as Beyond Hot Money (Dagher and Fuster, 2026). My job was to take ten U.S. banking datasets that each name a bank differently, resolve them to one identity per institution, and produce a panel clean enough to regress on.

1Ten sources, ten ways to name a bank

Every source identifies an institution with its own key, at its own level of the ownership tree. The work was deciding, pair by pair, what “the same bank” means, and making each decision auditable: every match carries a tier label, every ambiguous case is written out for inspection, every filter has a verification pass that prints what survived.

2Resolving 413 lender IDs to one institution each

The lines-of-credit file names lenders by a top-holder RSSD, but the numeric IDs sit at inconsistent depths of the ownership tree, so a flat join loses most of them. The fix was tiered: match at holding-company level first, then at bank level, then walk subsidiaries up the parent_id chain until they land in the Y-9C, then check the leftovers against the NCUA credit-union roster.1

Two more layers handled what tiered ID matching could not. Normalized fuzzy name matching (token_sort_ratio ≥ 80, after stripping punctuation and suffixes such as corp, inc, ltd) added matches against the Y-9C, the Call Report and the credit-union roster that no identifier could reach.2 Repeated self-joins of the NIC relationship file reached lead banks two and three levels down: of 134 holding companies in the credit file, 108 matched at the first level, 11 more at the second, 7 more at the third, and 8 stayed unresolved.3 Lenders whose RSSD changed in an acquisition were caught with the NIC predecessor → successor table.

# Walk a subsidiary up the parent_id chain until it lands in the FR Y-9C
def find_highest_parent_id(subsidiary_id, call_df, fr_report):
    def in_y9c(i):
        return i in fr_report['parent_id'].values or i in fr_report['id'].values

    current_id = subsidiary_id
    if in_y9c(current_id):
        return current_id
    while True:
        parent = call_df.loc[call_df['id'] == current_id, 'parent_id']
        if parent.empty:
            return None        # chain ends outside both filings
        current_id = parent.values[0]
        if in_y9c(current_id):
            return current_id

3Rolling the panel up and checking it against the filings

Holding companies do not report every variable the study needed, so subsidiary Call Report financials were rolled up the NIC ownership tree to recover them. The first obstacle was a convention, not missing data: in the 2017Q4 Y-9C file, 4,302 of 4,597 filers list no parent at all, which orphans banks from their holding company until parent_id is backfilled with the filer's own ID.4 The first merge on (parent_id, date) returned nothing for a similar reason: a string-versus-float mismatch on the join keys.

Rolled-up totals were then compared against three independent versions of the same quantity: the Y-9C consolidated figure, the FDIC figure, and the Call Report figure. Rows where the ratio of bank deposits to aggregated subsidiary deposits fell outside [0.9, 1.0] were written out for manual inspection, and the survivors were spot-checked against nine 2022 10-K filings (Bank of America, JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, U.S. Bank, M&T, SVB, TrustCo, Bank of Hawaii, Texas Capital). Five banks failed both the assets and the deposits check.4 The NY Fed CRSP–FRB link flagged 132 of the 413 lenders as publicly traded.1

4Twelve years of HMDA across the 2018 reporting break

HMDA changed its reporting standard in 2018: different column names, identifiers and codings. Before writing any filter, every column name for every year and file type was compiled into one table so the break was visible rather than discovered mid-merge.5

The filtered records (single-family, site-built, first lien, principal residence, purchase or refinance, originated) were collapsed to lender × MSA × year, given a jumbo flag from the FHFA conforming limits (which vary by year and county), attached to a top-holder RSSD, and joined to the bank panel. Each filtered year prints the unique values of its six filter columns; that verification pass is how a missing 2015 file was caught before it silently shortened the panel.5

5DealScan: can the data carry firm-time fixed effects?

Before anyone wrote a regression, the syndicated-loan data was tested for whether it has enough time variation and lender heterogeneity to identify firm-time fixed effects at the tranche or the deal level.6

441 of 28,568 deals (1.54%) show more distinct tranche-active dates than deal-input dates. A tranche cannot change without its parent deal being updated, so this is a recording gap in the source, not lender behaviour; used naively it would inflate the apparent time variation the fixed effects rely on.

6Replicating Choi, Goldsmith-Pinkham and Yorulmazer (2023)

To validate the deposit construction against a known benchmark, I reproduced Contagion Effects of the Silicon Valley Bank Run from the authors' R code, translating the data build into Python and regenerating Tables 1–6 and the figures (aggregate cumulative returns, 2022 and 2023 return densities, and the return-versus-vulnerability scatterplots). The Python FDIC deposit roll-up was diffed against the authors' R output file as the check that both pipelines agree.7

7Three things that looked like missing data and were not

  • Thousands of zero parent IDs in the Y-9C were a filing convention (a top holder lists no parent), not orphaned banks.
  • The 2018 HMDA break was a naming and coding change; the two eras had to be aligned field by field before any filter was written.
  • The 441 DealScan deals were a recording gap between two date fields, not real variation in lending.

The recurring lesson across all four datasets: most of what looks like missing data is a representation problem, and the value of the work was telling the two apart.
